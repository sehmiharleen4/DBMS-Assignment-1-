Graph Traversal and Relational Recursion:
What Published Benchmarks Show

Harleen Kaur, Gurveer Singh Brar, Hardik Jassar, Harmanparteek Singh
Department of Information Technology, Dr B. R. Ambedkar National Institute of Technology Jalandhar, Punjab, India
{kaurh.it.23, brargs.it.23, jassarh.it.23, singhhp.it.23}@nitj.ac.in
Faculty Mentor: Dr. Vijay Kumar
Abstract— Multi-hop traversal is a common operation in social networks and other connected data. This paper reviews published performance comparisons of graph and relational databases, with focus on Neo4j and recursive relational queries. We summarize a 2019 fairness study and a 2022 study of Neo4j, MySQL, and MariaDB. The reported results vary by query: Neo4j was about three times faster on two simple tasks in the 2022 study, while MariaDB was 29 times faster on one aggregation task and indexed relational systems were faster on the study's recursive invoice-chain query. A separate 2019 study found that indexes, cache state, and SQL formulation materially changed PostgreSQL's execution time. These results do not establish a current Neo4j-versus-PostgreSQL ranking for friends-of-friends traversal. The proposed G9 workload and code are described as a reproducible follow-up, but no local performance measurements are claimed here. The comparison therefore supports a workload-specific conclusion: query design and configuration affect the result, and published figures should not be transferred across different datasets or systems.
Keywords—graph database, property graph, Neo4j, recursive query, knowledge graph, PostgreSQL.

I. Introduction

Consider the “people you may know” panel of a social platform. To fill it, the system takes a user, collects that user's friends, then the friends of those friends, and ranks the newcomers. Fraud teams run the same pattern when they look for accounts that share devices within three hops of a flagged account, and supply-chain analysts run it to trace a faulty part through suppliers. In every case the cost of the query is dominated by traversal: following relationships repeatedly from a small set of starting entities.

A relational database stores a relationship as a row in an edge table, so each additional hop becomes another join. Graph databases take the opposite view: relationships are first-class objects stored next to the nodes they connect, and the engine follows them directly, an idea usually called index-free adjacency [11]. Vendors present this as a decisive advantage for multi-hop queries, while relational engines have meanwhile gained recursive queries (available in PostgreSQL since version 8.4) [22] and the SQL standard now includes graph pattern matching [9]. Earlier empirical comparisons [18] often used a single query formulation and software versions that are now several years old, so it is fair to ask how much of the advantage survives against a carefully written relational baseline.

Problem statement. What do published benchmarks establish about graph databases and relational recursion, and what remains unknown for Neo4j and PostgreSQL on friends-within-k-hops queries?

We answer three research questions:

  • RQ1: How do the property-graph and RDF models differ, and how do Cypher, GQL and SPARQL express graph patterns?
  • RQ2: How do native graph stores avoid costly joins, and how can a relational engine respond?
  • RQ3: What performance comparisons have published studies reported, and can those results answer the Neo4j-versus-PostgreSQL friends-within-k-hops question?

Contributions. (1) A comparison of property-graph and RDF models and their query languages. (2) A synthesis of published results about graph and relational query performance. (3) A reproducible design for a G9 friends-within-k-hops benchmark. We do not claim to have run that performance experiment.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section II gives background, Section III reviews related work, Sections IV and V describe the proposed benchmark method and setup, Section VI discusses published evidence, and Section VII concludes.

II. Background

Property graphs. A property graph consists of nodes and directed relationships. Nodes carry labels (for example Person); relationships have exactly one type (FRIEND); both nodes and relationships hold key–value properties [1], [3]. Fig. 1 shows a small example. RDF instead represents all data as subject–predicate–object triples identified by IRIs and queried with SPARQL [24]; attributes of a relationship need reification or similar devices.

Query languages. Cypher draws patterns as ASCII art, e.g. (a:Person)-[:FRIEND]-(b). GQL, standardised as ISO/IEC 39075:2024 [8], and SQL/PGQ, part 16 of SQL:2023 [9], share a common pattern-matching core [5], [6]; SQL/PGQ embeds it in SQL, whereas GQL is a stand-alone language.

Fig. 1. A property graph: labelled nodes, typed relationships, and properties on both.

Storage and traversal. In a relational design the edge table friendship(src,dst) is indexed on src; every hop performs index lookups (logarithmic in the table size) and a join. Native stores such as Neo4j keep, for each node, a reference to its first relationship, and relationships form linked lists, so locating the neighbours of a node does not depend on the total number of edges [11]. A depth-k query that must return the set of reachable persons is a breadth-first search costing O(|V|+|E|) in the worst case. If the engine instead enumerates paths, as the default relationship-uniqueness semantics of variable-length patterns suggests [23], the work grows roughly as dk for average degree d. Whether the planner can avoid path enumeration when only distinct end nodes are requested is therefore decisive, and we inspect plans with EXPLAIN (Section VI).

III. Related Work

Models and languages. Angles and Gutierrez survey graph data models [1], and a later survey covers the foundations of graph query languages [2]. Cypher's design and semantics are described by Francis et al. [3]. Recent work unifies pattern matching across GQL and SQL/PGQ [5], [6] and proposes schemas for property graphs [7]. Knowledge graphs, which use graph models at web scale, are surveyed in [4].

Systems. Besta et al. give a taxonomy of data organisation, system designs and queries in graph databases [10], and a community view lists open problems of big-graph systems [12]. Research systems explore adjacency-list indexing [14] and embedded graph engines [13].

Joins and relational engines. Worst-case optimal join algorithms show that classical binary joins can be asymptotically suboptimal on cyclic patterns [16]. Predefined joins make a relational engine competitive on graph workloads [15].

Benchmarks and comparisons. A study of social-network workloads reported that the advantage of specialised systems is not uniform [18]. The LDBC family supplies standard workloads [19], [20], [21].

Research gap. Table I shows that surveys and standards describe what graph systems offer, while comparisons rarely vary the relational formulation. We found no study that, with current releases, holds data and hardware fixed and compares a graph engine with both a naive recursive CTE and a level-wise relational baseline across depth, size and hub-versus-ordinary start nodes, with all scripts public. This paper fills that gap on a modest scale.

IV. Proposed Benchmark Method

A. Hypotheses

The proposed experiment uses four hypotheses. H1: at k=1 all systems may be within the same order of magnitude because the query performs little traversal. H2: recursive CTE latency may grow faster with k than Neo4j latency. H3: level-wise SQL may narrow any gap between a recursive CTE and Neo4j. H4: high-degree start nodes may increase latency. These remain untested.

C. Query Formulations

For start person s and depth k, every system returns the number of distinct persons other than s reachable by 1 to k friendship edges. PostgreSQL stores each friendship in both directions with a primary key on (src,dst); Neo4j stores it once and traverses it undirected. Listing 1 shows two formulations for k=3; the third is a chain of k joins with DISTINCT at every level. 

-- Variant A: Recursive CTE (PostgreSQL), k=3
WITH RECURSIVE r(id, depth) AS (
  SELECT dst, 1 FROM friendship WHERE src = :s
  UNION
  SELECT f.dst, r.depth + 1 FROM r
    JOIN friendship f ON f.src = r.id
  WHERE r.depth < 3)
SELECT count(DISTINCT id) FROM r WHERE id <> :s;

// Variant C: Cypher (Neo4j), k=3
MATCH (p:Person {id: $s})-[:FRIEND*1..3]-(f:Person)
WHERE f <> p RETURN count(DISTINCT f);
Listing 1. Recursive CTE (PostgreSQL) and Cypher (Neo4j) for k=3.

D. Experimental Controls

The planned run uses containers with matched CPU and memory limits. Each (depth, start) pair receives one untimed warm-up and five timed runs, including execution and result fetch. The analysis reports the median and 95th percentile and records statements that exceed a 60-second timeout. An offline self-test (selftest.py) passed for the SQL formulations against an independent Python breadth-first-search oracle over 136 query executions.

V. Planned Experimental Setup

Platform. The supplied Docker Compose file specifies PostgreSQL 16 (shared_buffers 2 GB) and Neo4j 5.26 Community (heap 2 GB, page cache 2 GB), each with an identical 4-CPU and 6-GB container limit. The benchmark harness uses Python 3 with psycopg2 and official Neo4j drivers.

Data. The generator uses synthetic data only, satisfying ethics guidelines. It creates Barabási–Albert graphs with each new person linking to five existing nodes. Table II shows intended sizes and edge counts.

B. RQ2: Avoiding Joins

Native stores resolve neighbours through stored memory pointers, while relational engines follow indexed edge rows using recursive CTEs or joins. Research on predefined joins and adjacency-list indexes demonstrates ways to close relational overhead [14], [15]. Query plans are decisive: without local EXPLAIN output, one cannot state which physical operators an optimizer selects under skewed degree distributions.

C. RQ3: Multi-Hop Query Latency

Published empirical studies demonstrate that database performance is highly dependent on query design, configuration, and hardware, rather than supporting a universal engine hierarchy:

Hohenstein and Jergler (2019) [25] inspected fairness in database comparisons. On a graph of 500,000 nodes with 50 edges each, for a 5-hop path query, adding a proper B-tree index on PostgreSQL reduced warm-start execution time from 60.25 seconds to 6.43 seconds. This demonstrates that relational query tuning and indexing can dramatically alter execution times by an order of magnitude.

Kotiranta et al. (2022) [26] compared Neo4j 4.4.8 Community with MySQL 8.0.29 and MariaDB 10.8.3 across various query workloads. While Neo4j was approximately 3× faster on two simple path traversals, MariaDB was 29× faster on an aggregation task. On a recursive invoice-chain query with 100 entities, indexed relational engines finished in ~1 ms compared to 42 ms in Neo4j. At 10,000 entities, MySQL was over 1,000× faster than Neo4j, while MariaDB completed in 10 ms.

Pacaci et al. (2017) [18] evaluated real-time social networking benchmarks and found that specialized graph engines do not uniformly outperform relational systems when relational queries are properly indexed and structured.

D. Threats to Validity

The cited studies use different database versions, hardware configurations, and query workloads. Kotiranta et al. evaluated MySQL and MariaDB rather than PostgreSQL, and their invoice-chain task differs from bounded social-graph reachability. Synthetic static graphs with warm caches also do not capture concurrent transactional read/write workloads or memory paging under constrained RAM.

VII. Conclusion and Future Work

Published empirical benchmarks show that graph databases can lead on deep traversal-heavy tasks, while relational databases match or outperform them on other analytical workloads when properly indexed and tuned. The literature does not support a blanket claim that native graph databases are always faster for social graph traversals. Our public benchmark harness and deterministic generator provide an open framework to execute this comparison directly on PostgreSQL 16 and Neo4j 5.26.
